Exam 2 mainly focuses on Four Key 
## 1. Elasticity
- four elasticities: Price Elasticity of Demand, Price Elasticity of Supply, Income Elasticity of Demand, and Cross-Price Elasticity
- Calculation: Know the Midpoint formula 

$$
\Large
E = \frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta P}
$$
$$
\Large
E_d = \frac{\dfrac{Q_2 - Q_1}{(Q_2 + Q_1)/2}}{\dfrac{P_2 - P_1}{(P_2 + P_1)/2}}
$$
- Interpreting Values: Elastic (>1), inelastic (<1), unit elastic (=1). For income elasticity, positive means a normal good and negative means an inferior good. For cross-price, positive means substitutes and negative means complements
- Determinants: Availability of Substitutes, necessity vs. luxury, time horizion, and share of the budget.
- Total Revenue Test: If demand is elastic, a price increase lowers revenue. If it's inelastic, a price increase raises revenue.
- Applications: Firm pricing decisions and government policy.

## 2. Welfare and Market Efficiency
- Consumer surplus: Willingness to pay minus price paid, the area under demand and above price.
- Producer Surplus: Price received minus cost, the area above supply and below price.
- Total Surplus: Consumer surplus plus producer surplus. It is maximized at the market equilibrium, which is why the equilibrium is efficient
-<font color="#9bbb59"> Positive vs. Normative: The module move from "what is the equilibrium?" to "is it the best outcome?"</font>

## 3. Price Ceilings and Price Floors
- Ceiling: A maximum price. It is binding only if set below equilibrium, and it creates a shortage.
- Floor: A minimum price. It is binding only if set above equilibrium, and it creates a surplus.
- Welfare effects: Know how to find the changes in consumer surplus and producer surplus and the deadweight loss (DWL) graphically.
- Real-World examples: Rent control and the minimum wage.

## 4. Taxes and Subsidies
- Tax incidence: Who actually bears the burden. It does not depend on which side of the market (buyer or seller) the tax is legally levied on. 
- Effects of a Tax: A wedge between the price buyers pay and the price sellers receive, a lower quantity traded, tax revenue, and DWL.
- Elasticity connection: The more inelastic side of the market bears more of the tax. DWL is larger when demand and supply are more elastic.
- Subsidies: The mirror image of taxes. They increase quantity, shift benefits to buyers and sellers, and also create DWL.
- Applications: Sin taxes and payroll taxes.

### Elasticity 
What it measures: How sensitive quantity demanded or supplied is to a change in price, income, or another good's price.

Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)
- Midpoint Formula:
$$
\Large
E_d = \frac{\dfrac{Q_2 - Q_1}{(Q_2 + Q_1)/2}}{\dfrac{P_2 - P_1}{(P_2 + P_1)/2}}
$$
- Demand slopes downward, so the result is negative. Use the absolute value when classifying


| \|$E_d$\| | Category            | Demand Curve          |
| --------- | ------------------- | --------------------- |
| 0         | Perfectly inelastic | Vertical              |
| 0<$E_d$<1 | Inelastic           | Steep                 |
| = 1       | Unit elastic        | Rectangular Hyperbola |
| > 1       | Elastic             | Flat                  |
| $\infty$  | Perfectly elastic   | Horizontal            |

Example: Price rises from $10 to $12 and quantity falls from 100 to 80.
- $\%\Delta Q$ =  -20 ÷ 90 = -22.2%
- $\%\Delta P$ = 2 ÷ 11 = 18.2%
- $E_d$ = -1.22, so |$E_d$| = 1.22 which is elastic
Determents (What makes demand more elastic):
- More close substitutes available
- Good is a luxury rather than a necessity
- Longer time horizon
- Larger share of the consumer's budget 
- Narrower market definition ("Korean BBQ" vs. "Food")

#### Total Revenue Test
$TR = P \times Q$

| Demand is... | Price Increase | Price Decrease |
| ------------ | -------------- | -------------- |
| Elastic      | TR falls       | TR rises       |
| Inelastic    | TR rises       | TR falls       |
| Unit elastic | No change      | No change      |
- On a linear demand curve, the upper half is elastic, the midpoint is unit elastic, and the lower half is inelastic. TR is maximized at the unit elastic point.
- Elasticity changes along a linear curve even though the slope is constant.

#### Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)
$$\Large E_s = \frac{\%\Delta Q_s}{\%\Delta P}$$

- Positive, because supply slopes upward.
- Determents
	- Time Horizon (longer is more elastic)
	- Availability of inputs
	- Spare production capacity 
	- Ability to store inventory
	- How easily firms can switch production

#### Income Elasticity of Demand 
$$\Large E_I = \frac{\%\Delta Q_d}{\%\Delta \text{Income}}$$
- $E_I$ > 0: Normal good.
	- If $E_I$ > 1 It is a luxury good
	- If 0 < $E_I$ < 1 It is a necessity
	- $E_I$ < 0 It is an inferior good

Applications to Know
- Firm pricing: Raises prices if demand is inelastic, cut them if demand is elastic.
- Agriculture: a bumper crop can lower farmers' revenue because demand for food is inelastic
- Government: Elasticity determines how much tax revenue a tax raises and how much it changes behavior

### Welfare and Market Efficiency
Big shift: From positive ("what is the equilibrium?") to normative ("is the equilibrium the best outcome?").

Main Vocab
- Willingness to pay: The maximum a buyer would pay. It is shown by the height of the demand curve
- Cost: The minimum a seller would accept: It is shown by the height of the supply curve
- Consumer Surplus: Willingness to pay - Price Paid. Graphically, it is the area below demand and above price
- Producer Surplus: Price received - cost. Graphically, it is the area above supply and below price.
- Total Surplus: Consumer Surplus + Producer Surplus, or Total Willingness to Pay - Total Cost For All Units Traded.

#### Calculating Surplus
For linear curves, Consumer Surplus and Producer Surplus are triangles:
$$\Large \text{Area} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$$
- Base = quantity traded
- Height = the gap between the curve's intercept and the price

Why Equilibrium Is Efficient? 
- Every unit where Willingness To Pay ≥ cost gets traded, so no gains from trade are left unrealized.
- No unit where cost > Willingness To Pay gets traded, so no value is destroyed.
- Total surplus is maximized at the competitive equilibrium
- Producing less than equilibrium leaves gains from trade unrealized, and producing more costs more than the value created. Either way surplus is lost (deadweight loss)

### Price Ceilings and Price Floors
Price Ceiling (legal maximum price)
- Binding only if set below equilibrium. Above equilibrium, it has no effect.
- Result: Quantity demanded > quantity supplied, which creates a shortage
	- Shortage = $Q_d - Q_s$
	- Quantity actually traded = $Q_s$ (the short side of the market)
- Welfare Effects:
	- Consumers who still get the good gain surplus (a rectangle from the lower price).
	- Producers lose surplus (that same rectangle plus part of their triangle)
	- Consumers who can no longer buy lose surplus
	- Dead weight loss is the triangle between the supply and demand curves from $Q_s$ to the equilibrium quantity
- Side effects: long waiting lines, search costs, black markets, lower quality, and non- price rationing
- Example: Rent Control

### Price Floor (legal Minimum Price)
- Binding only if set above equilibrium
- Result: Quantity supplied > quantity demanded, which creates a surplus.
	- Surplus = $Q_s - Q_d$
	- Quantity actually traded = $Q_d$ (the short side)
- Welfare Effects:
	- Producers who still sell gain from the higher price
	- Consumers lose surplus
	- Some producers lose surplus because they can't sell
	- Dead Weight Loss is the triangle from $Q_d$ to the equilibrium quantity
- Side Effects: wasted resources, excess production, and sellers competing on non-price factors
- Examples: Minimum wage (a floor in the labor market, so the surplus is unemployment) and agricultural price supports.

REMEMBER!
- Always find the equilibrium first, then compare the policy price to it
- The short side determines quantity traded
- The more elastic supply and demand are, the bigger the shortage or surplus and the larger the dead weight loss
- Be ready to name winners and losers and to explain the tradeoff between efficiency and the policy's goals

### Taxes and Subsidies
#### Tax Incidence 
- Statutory Incidence: Who is legally required to pay the tax
- Economic Incidence: Who actually bears the burden. The two can differ
- Key Fact: The burden is the same whether the tax is levied on buyers or sellers

#### Effects of a Per-Unit Tax t
- A tax on sellers shifts supply up by t. A tax on buyers shifts demand down by t. Either way gives the same result
- A wedge forms between what buyers pay and what sellers keep:
$$P_b - P_s = t$$
- Quantity traded falls below the equilibrium quantity
- Buyers' burden peer unit: $P_b - P^*$
- Sellers' burden per unit: $P^* - P_s$
- Tax revenue: $t \times Q_{tax}$ (a rectangle)
- Dead Weightless Loss: A triangle, $\tfrac{1}{2} \times t \times (Q^* - Q_{tax})$

Worked example: Demand is $P = 20 - Q$ and supply is $P = 2 + Q$, with a $2 tax
- Without the tax, $Q^* = 9$ and $P^* = 11$.
- With the tax, $(20 - Q) - (2 + Q) = 2$, so $Q_{tax} = 8$.
- $P_b = 12$ and $P_s = 10$, so buyers and sellers each bear $1 per unit.
- Tax revenue = $2 \times 8 = \$16$
- Dead Weight Loss = $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 2 \times (9 - 8) = \$1$

#### Elasticity and Incidence
- The more inelastic side of the market bears more of the tax, because it has fewer alternatives.
- Dead Weight Loss is larger when supply and demand are more elastic, because quantity responds more
- The most efficient way to raise revenue is to tax goods with inelastic demand and supply 

#### Subsidies (the mirror image of a tax)
- A per-unit subsidy s creates a wedge in the other direction: $P_s - P_b = s$
- Quantity traded rises above equilibrium
- Government cost: $s \times Q_{sub}$.
- Dead Weight Loss: A triangle, because units are produced where cost exceeds Willingness To Pay. 
- The benefit is split by elasticity: The more inelastic side gets more of it.
- The subsidy rewards who it is paid to and who it is not paid to equally, since incidence is the same either way.
